\title{How Muon Influences Each Part of an LLM}
\author{Vuk Rosi\'c}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Muon trains the hidden matrices of an LLM with an orthogonalized update and leaves the embedding, the output head and the normalization scales to Adam. We ask which of those matrices actually need Muon, and what each one needs from it. Starting from a tuned Muon baseline, we give one matrix, or one group of matrices, to Adam, choose learning rates on separate tune worlds, and score the change on held-out worlds that use different training and validation text. From width 64 to width 256 at 2,000 steps, giving the value and output matrices to Adam costs .0135, .0202 and .0403 in validation loss, the three feed-forward matrices together cost .0022, .0053 and .0111, and query and key cost .0002, $-.0056$ and .0012, so query and key stay near zero while the other two groups grow with width. The groups also want different step shapes. On value and output, keeping the gradient's own singular values as step sizes costs .0229 at width 48 and 250 steps. On query and key the same change costs nothing ($+.0029$ (.0013) at 250 steps, $+.0001$ (.0015) at 1,000), while shrinking their strong directions costs .0218. Removing QK-norm does not change this, and a one-step measure of loss on unseen batches does not explain it. Finally, we searched for a per-group step that beats tuned Muon, over 1,000 random update rules, published variants and hand-set spectrum shapes. The best, a step that gives value and output's weak directions two to three times the step of their strong ones, beats Muon walked over momentum and learning rate by .0019 at 1,000 steps on 10 of 10 held-out worlds, and by nothing at 4,000 steps; per-head Muon on query and key fades the same way. At these sizes, tuned Muon is not beaten by changing its step per group.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Muon~\cite{muon} replaces each hidden matrix's update with the nearest matrix that has all singular values equal to one. It is now used to train large models, yet an LLM holds several kinds of matrix that do different jobs: query, key, value and output matrices in attention, and gate, up and down matrices in a gated feed-forward layer. A practitioner who wants to change Muon, or to save its cost, needs to know which of these matrices carry its benefit.

Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025tail} answered the first version of this question. They start from Adam everywhere and add Muon to one group at a time at 160M parameters and 10,000 steps. Muon on the value and output matrices plus the feed-forward layer nearly matches Muon everywhere, and Muon on query and key alone adds little to Adam. Our results agree with that ranking.

This paper asks three questions that their design leaves open.

\begin{enumerate}
\item What does each matrix lose when it alone leaves a tuned Muon baseline? Adding Muon to Adam and removing it from Muon are different questions when matrices interact. In their gated table, Muon on any one feed-forward matrix lowers the loss by about .09 to .10 from Adam everywhere, while leaving one feed-forward matrix out of the full Muon set of value, output and feed-forward matrices costs .017 to .052.
\item Does the map survive tuning? Wang et al.\ keep the Muon learning rate fixed across their ablations and treat the remaining gap as possibly due to it. We retune the learning rate of every change on tune worlds and score it on other worlds.
\item How does the map move with width and with training length? Their numeric tables are at one size and one length.
\item What does each group need from Muon's step? We keep Muon's singular directions and change only their sizes, one group at a time.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{Model.} A transformer with RoPE, RMSNorm, normalized queries and keys, and a gated SiLU feed-forward layer, with no biases. A block with width $d$ holds $d\times d$ matrices $Q$, $K$, $V$ and $O$, $h\times d$ matrices gate and up, and a $d\times h$ matrix down, with $h=\lfloor 8d/3\rfloor$.

\paragraph{Worlds.} A world fixes the training text, the validation rows and the order of the data. Two worlds share no training or validation tokens. Worlds 0 to 4 choose settings, and worlds 5 to 9 report them. Five further worlds are sealed behind a separate program for later design claims and were not read for this paper.

\paragraph{Baseline.} Muon on the seven hidden matrix kinds and Adam on the embedding, head and normalization scales. In the small-model table, query and key use per-head Muon and the baseline's learning rate and momentum for each part are tuned together by coordinate descent on the mean of worlds 0 to 4. In the larger-model table, query and key use whole-matrix Muon, as in Wang et al., and the per-head version is a separate row.

\paragraph{The change.} One matrix kind, or a group of them, moves to Adam, and everything else stays as in the baseline. Adam's learning rate for the moved matrices is chosen on the tune worlds. We report the paired gap on held-out worlds, baseline loss minus changed loss, so a negative gap means Muon was better. The error is the standard error of the five per-world gaps.

Three things stand out at this size.

Value and output interact. At 250 steps, moving both costs .0209, while moving each alone costs .0076, a sum of .0152. The excess of .0057 is about 1.6 standard errors of the difference if the three gaps are treated as independent, so the interaction needs the larger runs below to confirm it.

Each feed-forward matrix alone is free to move. All three together cost .0041 at 250 steps, but they cost nothing at 1,000 steps at width 32, where the gap is $+.0012$ (.0015). Value and output carry most of what is left at 1,000 steps: moving the other five costs .0029 (.0009) and moving all seven costs .0176 (.0016).

Tuning Adam's first momentum at .5, .7 or .95 did not rescue the value or output matrix on the tune worlds, where the gaps stayed between $-.0073$ and $-.0083$ for each alone and between $-.0173$ and $-.0203$ for both.

\paragraph{Per-head Muon.} Orthogonalizing query and key per head rather than as whole matrices, as Kimi K3, GLM-5 and others do~\cite{perhead}, improved a five-world tuned row by .0095 (error .0024) on the held-out worlds, winning all 5, when both arms were tuned on worlds 0 to 4. The whole-matrix query and key setting inside an otherwise per-head tuned row scored $-.0046$ (.0020) at its best learning rate, so the split alone accounts for about half of that gap and the rest comes from retuning.

\paragraph{Variants of the update.} We also tested changes to the update against a tuned per-head row at the same size. None reached a gap of .005. Exact orthogonalization by SVD gave $+.0021$ (.0016). Raising singular values to a power hurt as the power grew, with $p=.25$ at $-.0049$ (.0016) and $p=.5$ at $-.0174$ (.0023). Removing the weak directions from the update cost $-.0170$ (.0009). Longer momentum on weak directions gave $-.0006$ to $-.0083$.

\section{Results across size and length}

Width 64 has 4 layers and 4 heads and 0.71M weights, width 128 has 6 layers and 8 heads and 2.2M weights, and width 256 has 6 layers and 8 heads and 6.8M weights.

The design is cheaper than at small size because of the GPU budget. Table~\ref{tab:map} gives the map. Three patterns hold across the sizes tested. Value and output are the group that matters most, and their cost roughly doubles with each doubling of width, from .0135 to .0202 to .0403. Query and key stay near zero, with query alone beating Muon by .0063 (.0012) at width 128 and .0032 (.0010) at width 256, in all 5 worlds at both. Each feed-forward matrix alone is free to move at every size and length, while the three together cost .0111 (.0014) at width 256, so they can substitute for one another up to a point.

Training length works against all of these. At width 64, moving all seven costs .0433 at 500 steps, .0119 at 2,000 and .0041 at 8,000, and no group stands out at 8,000 steps. Models with many tokens per weight may therefore show smaller gaps than short runs do, though Wang et al.'s gap of .3712 at 160M and 10,000 steps is much larger than any here, so length alone does not close it.

The row for all seven does not behave like the sum of its groups across sizes. At width 256 the three groups sum to .0526 against .0507, but at width 128 they sum to .0199 against .0511. Moved matrices inherit Adam learning rates from their groups in this design, which can overstate the cost of a joint move, so the width 128 excess needs its own tuned row before we read it as an interaction.

Per-head Muon on query and key beats whole-matrix Muon by .0052 (.0008) at width 128 and .0038 (.0015) at width 256, in all 5 worlds at both.

\paragraph{Adding Muon and removing it.} To compare with Wang et al.\ directly, we also run their design on our model with whole-matrix query and key. Adam trains every hidden matrix with one learning rate chosen on the tune worlds, and Muon then takes some matrices at the baseline's Muon learning rates. Table~\ref{tab:add} gives each group's gain from adding Muon and its cost when removed from full Muon, where removal uses that group's own tuned Adam learning rate. At both small sizes the two numbers nearly agree, and the added gains sum to .0297 against a full gain of .0265 at width 48 and to .0105 against .0147 at width 32. The groups therefore act close to independently at these sizes. Wang et al.'s gated table does not behave this way, since each feed-forward matrix added alone gains about .09 to .10 while removing one from the full set costs .017 to .052.

\paragraph{The learning rate on query and key.} At width 48, moving query and key to Adam from the full Muon baseline costs .0025 (.0009) at Adam's shared learning rate of .01 and gains .0023 (.0012) at their own tuned rate of .025, so that one learning rate sets the sign. At width 32 both rates are .01 and the gap is $+.0002$ (.0015). Wang et al.'s cost of .0187 is 5\% of their gap of .3712 between Adam and Muon everywhere, while the .0025 here is 9\% of .0265, so the two results differ less than the raw numbers suggest.

\paragraph{When the gain appears.} The gaps shrink during training. At width 128, moving all seven matrices to Adam costs about .186 at step 250 and about .056 at step 2,000, and moving value and output costs about .050 and .022. These curve values use only 64 validation rows at each mark, so they are noisier than the final gaps, which use 3,200 rows.


\section{What each group needs from the step}

We now keep the baseline fixed at width 48 and change only one group's step. The strongest quarter of a step's singular directions are its strong directions and the rest are its weak directions. Each change walks its own learning rate factor on worlds 0 and 1 and is scored on worlds 5 to 9. All predictions below were written before the runs and are in the repository's log.

\paragraph{Weak directions.} Table~\ref{tab:weak} removes the weak directions from one group's step, or keeps their size and pairs each weak left direction with the wrong right direction. Query and key lose little from either. Value and output and the feed-forward matrices lose about as much from scrambling as from removal, so these directions help through the information they carry and not only through their size. At 1,000 steps, removing them from all seven costs .0282, which is about twice the .0140 between Muon and Adam everywhere. A Muon step without weak directions is therefore worse than Adam, which keeps them. In that row all seven share one learning rate factor, x1, while feed-forward alone chose x4, so .0282 is an upper bound.

\paragraph{Step shapes.} Table~\ref{tab:shape} sets each direction's size to its singular value to the power $p$, scaled to Muon's step size, so $p=0$ is Muon and $p=1$ keeps the gradient's own shape, and also tries random sizes. Shumaylov et al.~\cite{shumaylov} report that random sizes match Muon on GPT-2, and we see the same in every group, within .0031. They also report that inverted sizes, $p=-1$, work as well on GPT-2. In our runs $p=-1$ is the worst shape in every group except feed-forward, where it ties the gradient's shape, and it costs .0900 on all seven. Our model is much smaller than theirs and we did not run theirs.

The two groups want opposite sides of Muon. Value and output cannot take the gradient's shape but can take mildly inverted sizes, while query and key can take the gradient's shape and cannot take inverted sizes. So value and output depend on weak directions keeping their size, and query and key depend on strong directions keeping theirs. Per-group shape is thus a real difference between the matrices, and it is not visible in a map that only swaps Muon for Adam.

\paragraph{Three explanations that failed.} We predicted that QK-norm causes query and key's indifference, since it removes their overall scale. Without QK-norm, moving query and key to Adam still gains .0023 (.0014) and removing their weak directions still gains .0010 (.0012), and value and output keep their costs, so QK-norm does not explain it.

We also predicted that Muon's step lowers the loss on unseen data faster than the gradient's shape does on value and output. We measured the cosine between a candidate step and the gradient of the next unseen batch at the same weights, in one training run. In every group the gradient's shape scores about three to five times higher than Muon's step, for example .2356 against .0504 on value and output at 250 steps, and the weak directions alone score almost nothing, .0023. So the help from weak directions is not a one-step drop in unseen loss. Value and output do have the most concentrated gradients, with .965 of the squared singular values in the strongest quarter against .925 for query and key and .876 for feed-forward, but feed-forward loses more than query and key from the gradient's shape, so concentration does not order the costs either. Why the groups differ remains open.

We then predicted that weak-direction steps keep value and output healthy by holding up the stable rank of the weights, the squared Frobenius norm over the squared largest singular value. In one training run at 1,000 steps, the gradient's shape does lower value's stable rank from 13.72 to 8.11 and output's from 12.00 to 5.15. But it lowers query's from 10.05 to 2.89 and key's from 7.90 to 3.45 with no loss cost, so low stable rank alone does not explain the cost on value and output.

We also gave each group its own step shape, choosing the exponent and learning rate on tune worlds. Together these gain $+.0052$ (.0008), 5 of 5 worlds, at 250 steps, but only $+.0028$ (.0011), 4 of 5 worlds, at 1,000 steps, so we treat this as a short-training effect. Worlds 5 to 9 have also scored many candidates by now, so even the 250-step number is not a clean test. No variant here passes our win line of .005 on all 5 worlds at both lengths.

\paragraph{A smaller, tilted step for value and output.} Many arms that gained about .002 on worlds 5 to 9 fell to about .001 or less on worlds 10 to 14, which no choice had looked at; for example the best of 40 exponent and learning rate cells on the feed-forward matrices fell from $+.0039$ to $+.0011$. One family held. On value and output only, with momentum .95, a step whose top quarter of directions is about a third of Muon's size and whose weakest quarter is two to three times larger than the top gains $+.0032$ (.0007) over tuned Muon at 1,000 steps, on 10 of 10 worlds (5 to 14). Muon itself, walked again on the same 10 worlds over momentum .8 to .95 and learning rate factors .35 to 1.4, gains at most $+.0013$. A flat step of the same top size gains $+.0014$, so the tilt toward weak directions adds about .0015. Giving each weak-quarter direction a random sign every step, which keeps its size, removes the whole gain ($-.0003$ (.0007)), so these directions carry information about where to go, which agrees with Table~\ref{tab:weak}. The gain needs momentum .95: the same step gains $-.0003$ at .8 and $+.0014$ at .9. The same shapes give no gain on the feed-forward matrices. At 250 steps the same step is again the best tried, $+.0024$ (.0013), but its neighbours are near 0, so the short run does not settle it. At 4,000 steps the edge is gone: the best tilted step gains $+.0025$ (.0008) and Muon at momentum .95 and a third of its tuned learning rate $+.0022$ (.0010); at momentum .97 Muon gains $+.0036$ and the tilt $+.0031$. At 1,000 steps Muon at momentum .97 gains at most $+.0007$, so the tilt's edge there holds across momentum .8 to .97, but it is gone by 4,000 steps, and we do not claim it as a method (Table~\ref{tab:tilt}). An exponent $c$ in $(GG^\top)^{-c}G$ between .6 and .7 gives this tilt, which is the opposite side of Muon from Muon$^p$~\cite{muonp}, whose powers lie between Muon and the gradient, and the same side as the inverted spectra of Shumaylov et al.~\cite{shumaylov} and the late negative powers of DynMuon~\cite{dynmuon}, though neither reports a per-group difference.

\section{Related work}

Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025tail} is the closest work, as described above. Compositional Muon~\cite{compositional} treats $QK^\top$ and $OV$ as composed circuits, with query and key handled per head with a half split, value per head and output as one matrix, and reports gains at 340M and 1B. Muon has also been ablated in vision transformers~\cite{muonvit}, where the largest difference is reported in query, key and value, which differs from the LLM ranking. CMuon~\cite{cmuon} ablates chunking across feed-forward, query-key-value and AdaLN matrices. Spectral Allocation~\cite{spectral} measures how often each matrix's update directions agree with gradients on unseen batches at one checkpoint, and reports that the weak directions of query and key agree least while feed-forward matrices stay aligned across almost the whole spectrum. Our next-batch cosines agree on feed-forward, whose weak directions score highest, and are too close to separate query and key from value and output. Su~\cite{su} shows in a curvature model that exact orthogonalization is an extreme case rather than a general optimum. Shumaylov et al.~\cite{shumaylov} test step shapes on the whole of GPT-2, and we test them per group.

\section{Limits}

These models have far fewer weights than those trained in practice and are trained for far fewer tokens. Width 256 has 6.8M weights and Wang et al.\ use 160M, so the map can still change at larger sizes. All losses are validation loss on next-token prediction, and we did not measure downstream tasks.

The larger-model baselines are tuned on two worlds and moved matrices inherit Adam learning rates from their group, as described above, so joint rows may overstate a cost. The baseline there uses whole-matrix query and key, so the per-head row is a change and not the reference. Tests of the step's shape run only at width 48, with 250 and 1,000 steps, and each uses three to seven learning rate factors on two worlds. The next-batch cosines come from one training run for each setting and have no error bars. Gaps smaller than about .004 are within noise at one world, and the win line we use is a gap above .005 with the same sign in all 5 held-out worlds.
